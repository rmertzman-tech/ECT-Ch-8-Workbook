Designing the rules before you know your seat

Before anyone has picked a room, roommates splitting an apartment reason differently about rent than they would once they know which room is theirs. This chapter takes that instinct to the scale of an entire institution's rules.

2 · Tuskegee

1932–1972

From 1932 to 1972, the U.S. Public Health Service enrolled 399 Black men who had syphilis and 201 men without it, telling them they were being treated for "bad blood." They were never told they had syphilis and never treated. When penicillin became the standard treatment in 1947, researchers withheld it — and coordinated with draft boards to prevent participants from receiving it as military inductees. The study continued 25 more years, ending only in 1972 when whistleblower Peter Buxtun leaked it to the press.

The researchers built trust deliberately — meals, burial insurance, a trusted nurse, Eunice Rivers — around a study the men never understood. Why did that trust make the deception more effective, not less?

3 · The Four Lenses

Four different questions about fairness

Rawls: Would this rule survive being chosen by someone who didn't yet know which position they'd occupy? (The difference principle: inequality is just only if it benefits the worst off.)

Nozick: Was the historical transaction that produced this outcome actually voluntary and legitimate? (His example: a basketball player earning $250,000 from a million voluntary quarters is not unjust — unless the history behind it wasn't actually voluntary.)

Nussbaum: Does a person have the actual, substantive capability to live with dignity — not merely the formal right to?

Honneth: Has this person's standing — through love, law, or social esteem — been recognized, or actively denied?

Nozick's framework depends entirely on the historical transaction being voluntary. Why can no version of his framework call Tuskegee's enrollment legitimate?

4 · Testimonial Injustice

When a person's own account can't count as evidence

Testimonial injustice: a person's account of their own experience given less credibility than it deserves because of prejudice against who they are, rather than anything wrong with the account itself.

The men had no route by which their own reported symptoms or confusion about "bad blood" could reach anyone with power to act on it. What does this add to the four lenses that they alone might miss?

5 · Running the Four Audits

Four instruments, not one

LensWhat It Reveals
RawlsNo one reasoning from behind a veil could rationally choose an arrangement where one group's survival is knowingly sacrificed to another's scientific curiosity.
NozickEnrollment through deliberate concealment is not a voluntary transaction — the very fact his framework requires was manufactured to look present while entirely absent.
NussbaumLife and bodily health, practical reason, and affiliation were all denied simultaneously.
HonnethLegal recognition and social esteem were both denied at the deepest level.

Tuskegee fails all four audits at once. What does that tell you about these four lenses — are they competing theories fighting for one right answer, or something else?

6 · The Bail Hearing

The same structure, today

A bail hearing takes twelve minutes. A man arrested on a nonviolent charge can't afford eight hundred dollars — ten percent of his eight-thousand-dollar bail. Over four months in jail awaiting trial, he loses his job, his apartment, and time with his children. He accepts a plea rather than wait longer.

Pick one of the four lenses and apply it to this case directly.
