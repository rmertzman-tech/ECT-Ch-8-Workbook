Instructor opening · 4–6 min
ORIENT — Chapter 8 after the reading

A rule does more than state a rule

You already know the simple Chapter 8 task: justice asks whether the structures people live under can be justified, while recognition asks whether people are treated as persons whose standing, capabilities, histories, and voices count. Today we treat an institution as an ongoing Doing .

Doing language: an institutional rule has an assembly history, present procedures and capacities, and forward effects. Once sedimented into forms, databases, software, habits, and authority, it can shape what later agents notice, what counts as evidence, and what future options remain open.
Today's critical-thinking problem: a record can support an administrative claim, but it does not automatically establish a just decision. Record ≠ whole situation. Rule compliance ≠ justice. Administrative authority ≠ unreviewable authority.
Opening question: “If a rule is applied exactly the same way to everyone, is that enough to make it fair?” Take 2–3 answers without resolving them.

Individual first · 5–6 min
ENTER — Preserve the first map

The Registration Hold

A college automatically blocks registration when a student's unpaid balance is more than $500. The rule is intended to make payment expectations clear and keep the college financially stable.

Maya has a $650 balance when registration opens. The system places a hold on her account, so she cannot register for next semester.

For now, assume only those facts.

Conversation setup · 6–7 min
MAP THE DOING — What is the institution actually doing?

From a rule to a task space

Conversation rule: do not start by deciding whether the college is good or bad. Trace the system. A rule becomes ethically important partly through what it allows, blocks, records, ignores, and makes hard to challenge.
Mapper
Trace record → classification → action.
Auditor
Ask which fairness test is relevant.
Voice-keeper
Ask whose account can enter as evidence.
License-keeper
Ask what justifies each transition.

3
Group jigsaw / solo audit · 14–16 min
FOUR AUDITS — Different questions, one institutional Doing

Do not vote on the theories; use them as instruments

Rawls — Position Audit

Would you accept the rule if you did not know which position you would occupy? How does it treat those with the least room to absorb the burden?

Nozick — History Audit

Was the transaction/history that produced the present claim legitimate and genuinely voluntary? What matters about how the balance arose?

Nussbaum — Capability Audit

What substantive capability does the person actually retain or lose? A formal right is not the same as a real ability to act.

Honneth — Recognition Audit

Is the person treated as a rights-bearing participant with standing, or reduced to a status/category that erases social and personal recognition?

Instructor reveal: Maya's balance exists because her financial aid was delayed during a college verification review. She has already completed the requested paperwork. If she cannot register this week, a required course may fill and delay graduation by a semester.
Conversation checkpoint: Where do the four audits converge? Where do they ask genuinely different questions? Convergence strengthens the diagnosis of this case; it does not make the four theories identical.
4
Recognition + evidence channel · 9–10 min
VOICE — Can Maya's account function as evidence?

Recognition is partly about whether the system can hear

New information: Maya contacted the billing office twice and explained that the college's own verification process delayed her aid. The account screen still says only Balance due: $650 . At the counter she is told, “Everyone has a reason. The system won't let me remove the hold.” The formal appeal will be reviewed after registration closes.
Testimonial injustice: a person's account is given less credibility than it deserves because of prejudice or status rather than because the account itself is defective. In EHI terms, we can ask a more general question too: does this person's account have a legitimate route into the evidence used by the decision process?
Claim ceiling: giving Maya testimonial standing does not mean her account must automatically control the decision. It means her account must be able to enter the inquiry as evidence and receive appropriate evaluation.
5
Historical pressure test · 6–8 min
TUSKEGEE — Why trust can deepen injustice

Use the history to pressure-test the architecture

In the Chapter 8 workbook, the Tuskegee case is used to show how deception, institutional authority, and deliberately cultivated trust could work together. The men were told they were being treated for “bad blood”; the workbook emphasizes that the structure denied genuine consent and blocked their own experience from functioning effectively as evidence within the system.

Do not treat Maya's case as “the same as Tuskegee.” The point of comparison is structural: what happens when institutional maps, authority, and evidence channels make affected people unable to challenge the representation being used to govern them?

6
EHI critical-thinking seam · 9–10 min
LICENSE — Where does the reasoning jump?

From database record to authorized institutional action

Record → Classification → Rule → Recommendation → Authorization → Action
Motivated firewalls: record ≠ whole situation ; formal equality ≠ equal capability ; policy compliance ≠ justice ; testimony ≠ automatic truth, but it may have standing as evidence ; administrative authority ≠ unreviewable authority .
Whole-class board: write three columns: System knows / Maya knows / Decision-maker still needs . Ask whether the missing information is a data problem, a standing problem, a timing problem, a rule-design problem—or several at once.

TRANSFER — A bail decision

Can you carry the architecture into a different institution?

A person arrested on a nonviolent charge is assigned bail that requires $800 cash to secure release. They cannot pay it. While awaiting trial, they risk losing work, housing, and time with their children. The hearing is brief, and the decision-maker has little information about those consequences.
